Scoring the steps, not just the answer

An outcome reward model, the Best-of-N tab's verifier, gives a whole solution one number. A process reward model (PRM) gives a number after every step: its estimate that the solution is still on a right track at that point. That says where a solution went wrong, not only whether it did, and it can score a solution that is not finished yet, which the Tree search tab relies on.

Uesato et al. ran the first careful comparison of the two kinds of supervision on GSM8K and found that outcome supervision gave about as few wrong final answers with fewer labels, but that right reasoning steps needed process supervision or a reward model that imitates it. Lightman et al. then found process supervision clearly better than outcome supervision for choosing among samples on the harder MATH problems. On this page Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-Qwen-2.5-1.5B, fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, scores every step of the Best-of-N tab's 16 answers to each of GSM8K test rows 0–39.

Every step scored

A step here is a line. The input is the question and then the answer split at every newline, each line followed by a newline token, as Skywork's inference code prepares it. The model is a Qwen2 decoder with a value head, a linear layer on its last hidden state; at the newline token that ends step t it gives

rt​=σ(w⊤ht​+b)∈(0,1),

read as the probability that the solution is still correct after that step. The Every step scored panel draws each step's rt​ as a bar, tinted red under the threshold of 0.5; blank lines get scores too and are left out. Because the decoder is causal, ht​ depends only on the text up to step t: the page checks that a prefix of an answer gets the same scores for its lines as the whole answer does, to 9×10−7.

The first step under 0.5 is where the PRM says the solution went wrong. Of the 130 wrong answers, 105 have such a step; so do 92 of the 510 right ones. A flag is a strong sign of trouble, not proof.

From steps to one score

To pick one answer of 16, the step scores of each answer r1​,…,rT​ must become one number. The From steps to one score panel shows four ways for every answer:

smin​=tmin​rt​,slast​=rT​,sprod​=t=1∏T​rt​,smean​=T1​t=1∑T​rt​.

Which one fits depends on what rt​ means. Lightman et al. score each step's own correctness, so the product is the probability that every step is right. Skywork's rt​ is already cumulative, the chance that everything so far is right, so the last score is the PRM's estimate for the whole solution and the minimum is its weakest point; the product then counts the early steps again and again, and it shrinks with the number of steps whatever they say. The mean, which Skywork's model card uses for best-of-N, lets many easy steps outweigh one bad one.

Against the outcome reward model

The Best-of-N over 40 problems panel uses each aggregate as the Best-of-N tab uses the outcome reward model, with the same exact average over all subsets of the 16 answers. With all 16, the PRM's last step is right on 90.0 % of the problems, its minimum on 87.5 %, the same as the outcome reward model, and its product and mean on 82.5 %, below the majority vote's 83.8 %.

The table's second column asks a different question: of the 652 pairs of a right and a wrong answer to the same problem, how often does the score put the right one higher? The outcome reward model does best there, 77.9 %, then the PRM's last step at 74.7 %; the minimum, mean and product order between 66.9 % and 70.2 % of pairs correctly. Choosing the single best answer and ordering all of them are different tasks, and best-of-N only needs the first.

How process reward models are trained

Step labels are expensive. Lightman et al. had people label about 800,000 steps of model-written MATH solutions as positive, negative or neutral (PRM800K). Math-Shepherd labels steps automatically instead: from each step it lets a model finish the solution several times and, in its main variant, marks the step good if any completion reaches the right answer. Zhang et al., from the Qwen team, found such Monte Carlo labels noisier than labels from human annotators or a strong model acting as judge, and warned that judging a PRM by best-of-N accuracy rewards PRMs that accept right answers reached by flawed steps. ProcessBench measures the other skill directly: finding the earliest wrong step.
