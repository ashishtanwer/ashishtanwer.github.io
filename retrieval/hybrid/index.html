Two retrievers that fail differently

Hybrid search runs a lexical retriever and a dense one side by side and merges their results. They fail on different questions: BM25 matches exact strings, so it finds a rare name or number but misses a paraphrase; a dense model matches meaning, so it finds the paraphrase but can blur two similar names. Merging the lists keeps what each gets right.

This tab fuses the BM25 tab's ranking and the Embeddings tab's bge-small-en-v1.5 ranking of the same 2,067 SQuAD paragraphs, each method's top 100 for each of the 300 questions. No model runs here: the lists are the two tabs' recordings.

Why the scores cannot be added

The Two ranked lists panel shows both top 10s for the selected question, each paragraph tagged with its rank in the other list. BM25 scores are unbounded sums of term weights (16.6 for the first question's best paragraph); bge-small's are cosines, between 0.67 and 0.77 for that question's top ten. Adding them would let BM25 decide alone, and how far apart two scores are means different things on the two scales. A fusion rule has to make them comparable first, or ignore the scores altogether.

Reciprocal rank fusion

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores the scores and uses only ranks. A paragraph ranked rm​(d) by method m gets

RRF(d)=m∑​k+rm​(d)1​,

summed over the lists that hold it; a list that does not hold it adds nothing. The constant k (60 in the original paper) flattens the reward for being near the top, so a paragraph ranked well by both methods beats one ranked first by only one: with k=60, first place alone scores 1/61≈0.0164, second place in both 2/62≈0.0323. The Fused by reciprocal rank bars split each fused score into its two parts.

With only two lists, RRF produces exact ties: a paragraph first in BM25 and absent from bge-small's top 100 scores exactly the same as one first in bge-small and absent from BM25's. For 24 of the 300 questions the relevant paragraph shares its fused score with another paragraph, and the order between them is just corpus order. Depending on how those ties fall, RRF's Recall@1 at k=60 lies between 0.737 and 0.797; with ties broken by paragraph id it is 0.773.

Normalized scores and a weighted sum

The alternative keeps the scores but rescales each list to 0 to 1 by its own minimum and maximum (min–max normalization), then takes a convex combination:

s(d)=αs^dense​(d)+(1−α)s^BM25​(d),

where a paragraph outside a list gets 0 from it. α=0 is BM25's order and α=1 bge-small's. Unlike RRF it keeps how far ahead the first result is: a paragraph that one method scores far above the rest stays ahead. The strip's chart draws nDCG@10 over α; here it peaks at α=0.5 with 0.899, Recall@1 0.813.

Bruch and colleagues found the same on several benchmarks: a convex combination of normalized scores beat RRF in their experiments, and needs few labelled queries to choose α. One caution applies to every number in this tab's charts: α and k are swept on the same 300 questions they are scored on, so the best setting is tuned on its own test set.

What fusion recovers

The Which questions each gets right table sorts the questions by whether BM25 and bge-small put the relevant paragraph first. Both do for 167, and so do both fused lists. Only BM25 does for 53 questions, of which RRF (k=60) keeps 40 and the convex combination (α=0.5) 47; only bge-small does for 35, of which they keep 17 and 23; neither does for 45, and fusion still puts it first for 8 and 7 of them. Recall@1 rises from 0.733 (BM25) and 0.673 (bge-small) to 0.773 (RRF) and 0.813 (convex); nDCG@10 from 0.838 and 0.810 to 0.875 and 0.899. In the top 10 only one question is lost by both methods, and neither fused list brings it back.

The RRF chart shows the effect of k: a smaller k trusts the first places more. Here k=5 gives nDCG@10 0.892 and k=60 0.875; from k=200 on the metrics no longer change.

Where it came from

Fusing ranked lists is older than neural retrieval: metasearch engines combined the results of several engines, and Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher proposed RRF in 2009, showing that this one-line rule beat Condorcet voting and learned rank combinations on TREC runs. When dense passage retrieval arrived, Karpukhin and colleagues already reported that adding BM25's scores helped DPR on some datasets, and later work on hybrid retrieval (Kuzi et al., Ma et al., Chen et al.) found the gains largest out of domain. Bruch, Gai and Ingber's analysis compared the fusion functions directly. Search engines now ship both rules; the Reranker tab adds a third stage that reads the question with each fused candidate.

Papers

  1. Reciprocal rank fusion outperforms condorcet and individual rank learning methods Gordon V. Cormack, Charles L. A. Clarke, Stefan Büttcher · SIGIR 2009 Introduces reciprocal rank fusion, Σ 1 / (k + rank) with k = 60, the rule this tab draws.
  2. An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval Sebastian Bruch, Siyu Gai, Amir Ingber · arXiv 2022 Compares RRF with a convex combination of normalized scores and finds the convex combination better and easy to tune.
  3. Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering Vladimir Karpukhin et al. · EMNLP 2020 The dense retriever that set the modern pattern, with an early BM25 + dense hybrid among its results.
  4. A Replication Study of Dense Passage Retriever Xueguang Ma, Kai Sun, Ronak Pradeep, Jimmy Lin · arXiv 2021 Reproduces DPR and finds that a properly normalized hybrid with BM25 beats either retriever alone.
  5. Leveraging Semantic and Lexical Matching to Improve the Recall of Document Retrieval Systems: A Hybrid Approach Saar Kuzi, Mingyang Zhang, Cheng Li, Michael Bendersky, Marc Najork · arXiv 2020 Shows that lexical and semantic retrievers return largely different relevant documents, so their union raises recall.
  6. Out-of-Domain Semantics to the Rescue! Zero-Shot Hybrid Retrieval Models Tao Chen, Mingyang Zhang, Jing Lu, Michael Bendersky, Marc Najork · ECIR 2022 Finds hybrid lexical + dense retrieval most useful out of domain, where dense models alone degrade.
  7. C-Pack: Packed Resources For General Chinese Embeddings Shitao Xiao, Zheng Liu, Peitian Zhang, Niklas Muennighoff, Defu Lian, Jian-Yun Nie · arXiv 2023 The BGE embedding models, including bge-small-en-v1.5, the dense side of this tab.
